# Electricity: Complete Study Notes (Class 10 Level)

## 1. Introduction to Electricity

Electricity is a form of energy that is produced by the movement or the presence of electric charges.
Electricity is used in homes, schools, hospitals, factories, and transport systems all over the world.
Electricity is one of the most convenient forms of energy because electricity can be transported easily through wires.
Electricity can be converted into light energy in an electric bulb.
Electricity can be converted into heat energy in an electric heater.
Electricity can be converted into mechanical energy in an electric fan and an electric motor.
Electricity can be converted into sound energy in a loudspeaker.
Electricity can be produced from many sources such as coal, water, wind, sunlight, and nuclear fuel.
A power station is a place where electricity is generated on a large scale.
Electricity is carried from power stations to cities through a network of cables called the power grid.

## 2. Electric Charge

Electric charge is a basic property of matter that causes matter to experience a force in an electric field.
The SI unit of electric charge is the coulomb, and the symbol of coulomb is C.
There are two types of electric charge, which are positive charge and negative charge.
A proton carries a positive charge, and an electron carries a negative charge.
A neutron carries no electric charge, so a neutron is electrically neutral.
The charge on one electron is equal to about 1.6 times 10 to the power minus 19 coulomb.
One coulomb of charge is equal to the charge of about 6 times 10 to the power 18 electrons.
Like charges repel each other, which means two positive charges push each other away and two negative charges push each other away.
Unlike charges attract each other, which means a positive charge and a negative charge pull each other closer.
Charge is conserved, which means charge can neither be created nor destroyed but can only be transferred from one body to another.
A glass rod becomes positively charged when a glass rod is rubbed with silk because electrons move from the glass rod to the silk.
An ebonite rod becomes negatively charged when an ebonite rod is rubbed with fur because electrons move from the fur to the ebonite rod.
Static electricity is the electricity produced by rubbing, and static electricity does not flow continuously.

## 3. Electric Current

Electric current is defined as the rate of flow of electric charge through a conductor.
Electric current is represented by the letter I.
The formula for electric current is current equals charge divided by time, which is written as I equals Q divided by t.
In the formula for electric current, Q is the charge in coulombs and t is the time in seconds.
The SI unit of electric current is the ampere, and the symbol of ampere is A.
One ampere of current means that a charge of one coulomb flows through a cross-section of a conductor in one second.
Smaller currents are measured in milliampere, where one milliampere is equal to one thousandth of an ampere.
Even smaller currents are measured in microampere, where one microampere is equal to one millionth of an ampere.
Electric current is measured by an instrument called an ammeter.
An ammeter is always connected in series in an electric circuit.
An ammeter has very low resistance so that an ammeter does not change the current in the circuit.
Electric current is a scalar quantity because electric current has magnitude but follows no vector addition rules.

### Direction of electric current

The conventional direction of electric current is from the positive terminal of the cell to the negative terminal of the cell outside the cell.
The actual direction of electron flow is from the negative terminal of the cell to the positive terminal of the cell.
Conventional current and electron flow therefore move in opposite directions.
The conventional direction of current was chosen before the discovery of the electron.

### Types of current

Direct current, also called DC, is current that flows in only one direction.
A cell and a battery supply direct current.
Alternating current, also called AC, is current that reverses its direction periodically.
The electricity supplied to homes in India is alternating current with a frequency of 50 hertz.
Alternating current changes its direction 100 times in one second when the frequency is 50 hertz.

## 4. Electric Potential and Potential Difference

Electric potential at a point is the work done in bringing a unit positive charge from infinity to that point.
Potential difference between two points is defined as the work done to move a unit charge from one point to the other point.
The formula for potential difference is potential difference equals work done divided by charge, which is written as V equals W divided by Q.
The SI unit of potential difference is the volt, and the symbol of volt is V.
One volt is the potential difference between two points when one joule of work is done to move one coulomb of charge between the two points.
Potential difference is measured by an instrument called a voltmeter.
A voltmeter is always connected in parallel across the component whose potential difference is to be measured.
A voltmeter has very high resistance so that a voltmeter does not draw current from the circuit.
Potential difference acts like electrical pressure that pushes charges through a conductor.
A cell maintains a potential difference between its two terminals through a chemical reaction inside the cell.
Current flows in a circuit only when there is a potential difference between two points of the circuit.
Water flowing from a higher level to a lower level is a common analogy for current flowing because of a potential difference.

## 5. Electric Circuit and Its Components

An electric circuit is a continuous and closed path through which electric current flows.
A closed circuit is a circuit in which the path is complete, and current flows in a closed circuit.
An open circuit is a circuit in which the path is broken, and no current flows in an open circuit.
A cell is a source that converts chemical energy into electrical energy.
A battery is a combination of two or more cells connected together.
A switch is a device that opens or closes a circuit to stop or allow the flow of current.
A resistor is a component that opposes the flow of current in a circuit.
A rheostat is a variable resistor whose resistance can be changed to control the current in a circuit.
A fuse is a safety device that melts and breaks the circuit when the current becomes too high.
A bulb is a device that converts electrical energy into light and heat.
Connecting wires are made of copper or aluminium because copper and aluminium have low resistance.
A circuit diagram is a drawing that shows the components of a circuit using standard symbols.

## 6. Ohm's Law

Ohm's law states that the current flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across the conductor, provided the temperature and other physical conditions remain constant.
Ohm's law was stated by the German scientist Georg Simon Ohm in the year 1827.
The mathematical form of Ohm's law is V equals I multiplied by R.
In Ohm's law, V is the potential difference in volts, I is the current in amperes, and R is the resistance in ohms.
A graph of potential difference against current for a conductor that obeys Ohm's law is a straight line passing through the origin.
The slope of the voltage versus current graph gives the resistance of the conductor.
A conductor that obeys Ohm's law is called an ohmic conductor.
A conductor that does not obey Ohm's law is called a non-ohmic conductor.
A diode and a filament of a bulb at high temperature are examples of non-ohmic conductors.
Ohm's law is valid only when the temperature of the conductor stays constant.
If the potential difference across a resistor is doubled, the current through the resistor is also doubled.

## 7. Resistance

Resistance is the property of a conductor by which the conductor opposes the flow of electric current through the conductor.
Resistance is represented by the letter R.
The SI unit of resistance is the ohm, and the symbol of ohm is the Greek letter omega.
One ohm is the resistance of a conductor when a current of one ampere flows through the conductor and the potential difference across the conductor is one volt.
Resistance is caused by the collisions of free electrons with the atoms of the conductor.

### Factors affecting the resistance of a conductor

The resistance of a conductor is directly proportional to the length of the conductor, so a longer wire has more resistance.
The resistance of a conductor is inversely proportional to the area of cross-section of the conductor, so a thicker wire has less resistance.
The resistance of a conductor depends on the material of the conductor.
The resistance of a conductor depends on the temperature of the conductor.
The resistance of most metals increases when the temperature of the metal increases.
The formula for resistance in terms of length and area is R equals rho multiplied by length divided by area, which is written as R equals rho L divided by A.
In the formula for resistance, rho is the resistivity of the material, L is the length of the wire, and A is the area of cross-section of the wire.

## 8. Resistivity

Resistivity is the resistance of a conductor of unit length and unit area of cross-section.
Resistivity is also called specific resistance.
The SI unit of resistivity is the ohm metre, and the symbol of ohm metre is the Greek letter omega multiplied by m.
Resistivity depends on the material and the temperature of the conductor, but resistivity does not depend on the length or the area of cross-section of the conductor.
Silver and copper have very low resistivity, so silver and copper are very good conductors of electricity.
Alloys such as nichrome, manganin, and constantan have higher resistivity than pure metals.
Nichrome is used in heating elements of electric heaters, toasters, and irons because nichrome has high resistivity and does not oxidise easily at high temperatures.
Tungsten is used for the filament of an electric bulb because tungsten has a very high melting point.
Alloys are used in standard resistors because the resistivity of alloys changes very little with temperature.
Insulators such as rubber and glass have extremely high resistivity.

## 9. Resistors in Series

Resistors are said to be in series when resistors are connected end to end so that the same current flows through each resistor.
In a series combination, the current is the same through every resistor.
In a series combination, the total potential difference is the sum of the potential differences across each resistor.
The equivalent resistance of resistors in series is equal to the sum of the individual resistances.
The formula for the equivalent resistance in series is R equals R1 plus R2 plus R3.
The equivalent resistance in series is always greater than the largest individual resistance.
If one component in a series circuit fails, the entire circuit stops working because the circuit becomes open.
Series circuits are used in decorative lights, but series circuits are not used in houses because one faulty bulb would switch off all the bulbs.

## 10. Resistors in Parallel

Resistors are said to be in parallel when the first ends of all the resistors are joined together and the second ends of all the resistors are joined together.
In a parallel combination, the potential difference is the same across every resistor.
In a parallel combination, the total current is the sum of the currents through each resistor.
The reciprocal of the equivalent resistance in parallel is equal to the sum of the reciprocals of the individual resistances.
The formula for the equivalent resistance in parallel is one divided by R equals one divided by R1 plus one divided by R2 plus one divided by R3.
The equivalent resistance in parallel is always less than the smallest individual resistance.
In a parallel circuit, each appliance works independently, so the failure of one appliance does not stop the other appliances.
Parallel connection is used in household wiring because each appliance receives the same voltage and each appliance can be switched on or off separately.
The resistor with the smaller resistance carries a larger current in a parallel combination.

## 11. Heating Effect of Electric Current

The heating effect of electric current is the production of heat in a conductor when electric current flows through the conductor.
The heating effect of current occurs because the electrons collide with atoms and transfer kinetic energy to the atoms.
Joule's law of heating states that the heat produced in a resistor is directly proportional to the square of the current, the resistance, and the time for which the current flows.
The formula for Joule's law of heating is H equals I squared multiplied by R multiplied by t.
In Joule's law of heating, H is the heat produced in joules, I is the current in amperes, R is the resistance in ohms, and t is the time in seconds.
If the current through a resistor is doubled, the heat produced in the resistor becomes four times as large.
An electric heater, an electric iron, a toaster, and an electric kettle work on the heating effect of current.
An electric bulb also works on the heating effect of current because the filament becomes white hot and gives out light.
A fuse works on the heating effect of current because a fuse wire melts when too much current flows through the fuse wire.
A fuse wire is made of an alloy of tin and lead because the alloy has a low melting point.
Heating effect of current is an unwanted effect in devices such as computers, televisions, and electric motors because heat wastes energy.

## 12. Electric Power and Electrical Energy

Electric power is defined as the rate at which electrical energy is consumed or work is done in an electric circuit.
Electric power is represented by the letter P.
The formula for electric power is P equals V multiplied by I.
Electric power can also be written as P equals I squared multiplied by R.
Electric power can also be written as P equals V squared divided by R.
The SI unit of electric power is the watt, and the symbol of watt is W.
One watt is the power consumed when a current of one ampere flows under a potential difference of one volt.
One kilowatt is equal to 1000 watts.
Electrical energy is the total work done by the electric current in a given time, and electrical energy equals power multiplied by time.
The formula for electrical energy is E equals P multiplied by t, which is also E equals V multiplied by I multiplied by t.
The SI unit of electrical energy is the joule.
The commercial unit of electrical energy is the kilowatt hour, and the kilowatt hour is also called one unit of electricity.
One kilowatt hour is the energy consumed when an appliance of power one kilowatt works for one hour.
One kilowatt hour is equal to 3.6 times 10 to the power 6 joules.
Electricity bills in homes are calculated by multiplying the number of units consumed by the cost of one unit.
An electric meter measures the electrical energy consumed in a house in kilowatt hours.
A bulb marked 60 watt and 220 volt consumes 60 joules of energy every second when the bulb is connected to a 220 volt supply.

## 13. Domestic Electric Circuits and Safety

Domestic electric supply in India is alternating current at a potential difference of 220 volts and a frequency of 50 hertz.
Domestic wiring uses three wires, which are the live wire, the neutral wire, and the earth wire.
The live wire is the wire that carries current from the power station, and the live wire is usually covered with red insulation.
The neutral wire is the wire that carries the current back to the power station, and the neutral wire is usually covered with black insulation.
The earth wire is a safety wire that is connected to a metal plate buried deep in the ground, and the earth wire is usually covered with green insulation.
The potential difference between the live wire and the neutral wire is 220 volts.
The earth wire provides a low resistance path for current to flow into the ground if a metal appliance becomes live.
Earthing protects the user from electric shock by sending leakage current into the ground.
Earthing is important for appliances with metal bodies such as refrigerators, washing machines, and electric irons.
A fuse is connected in series with the live wire so that a fuse can break the circuit when the current becomes too high.
Overloading occurs when too many appliances are connected to a single socket, and overloading causes a very large current to flow in the wire.
Short circuiting occurs when the live wire and the neutral wire touch each other directly, and short circuiting causes a very large current because the resistance becomes very small.
A miniature circuit breaker, also called an MCB, is a modern device that switches off the circuit automatically when overloading or short circuiting occurs.
A miniature circuit breaker can be reset after the fault is removed, but a fuse wire must be replaced after the fuse wire melts.
Wet hands should never touch electrical switches or plugs because water is a good conductor of electricity and can cause a fatal electric shock.

## 14. Conductors, Insulators, and Semiconductors

A conductor is a material that allows electric current to pass through the material easily.
Metals such as copper, silver, aluminium, and iron are good conductors of electricity.
Metals are good conductors because metals contain a large number of free electrons.
An insulator is a material that does not allow electric current to pass through the material easily.
Rubber, plastic, glass, dry wood, and porcelain are examples of insulators.
Insulators do not conduct electricity because insulators have almost no free electrons.
A semiconductor is a material whose conductivity lies between the conductivity of a conductor and the conductivity of an insulator.
Silicon and germanium are common examples of semiconductors.
The conductivity of a semiconductor increases when the temperature of the semiconductor increases.
Semiconductors are used to make diodes, transistors, and computer chips.
Pure water is a poor conductor of electricity, but water containing dissolved salts is a good conductor of electricity.

## 15. Measuring Instruments in Electric Circuits

An ammeter is an instrument used to measure the current in a circuit, and an ammeter is connected in series.
A voltmeter is an instrument used to measure the potential difference between two points, and a voltmeter is connected in parallel.
A galvanometer is an instrument used to detect the presence of a small current in a circuit.
An ideal ammeter has zero resistance, and an ideal voltmeter has infinite resistance.
A multimeter is an instrument that can measure current, voltage, and resistance in a single device.
The positive terminal of an ammeter or a voltmeter must be connected towards the positive terminal of the cell.

## 16. Sources of Electricity

A cell is a device that converts chemical energy into electrical energy.
A dry cell is a primary cell that cannot be recharged after the chemicals are used up.
A lead acid battery is a secondary cell that can be recharged many times.
The potential difference of a single dry cell is about 1.5 volts.
A generator converts mechanical energy into electrical energy by rotating a coil in a magnetic field.
A solar cell converts light energy from the sun directly into electrical energy.
A thermal power plant produces electricity by burning coal to heat water and produce steam that turns a turbine.
A hydroelectric power plant produces electricity by using the energy of falling water to turn a turbine.
A wind turbine produces electricity by using the kinetic energy of moving air to turn a generator.

## 17. Solved Numerical Problems

### Problem 1: Finding current from charge and time

A charge of 600 coulombs flows through a wire in 2 minutes, and the question asks for the current in the wire.
The time of 2 minutes is converted into 120 seconds.
The current is found by dividing the charge by the time, which gives 600 divided by 120 equal to 5 amperes.
The current in the wire is therefore 5 amperes.

### Problem 2: Finding resistance using Ohm's law

A potential difference of 12 volts is applied across a resistor, and a current of 3 amperes flows through the resistor.
The resistance is found by dividing the potential difference by the current, which gives 12 divided by 3 equal to 4 ohms.
The resistance of the resistor is therefore 4 ohms.

### Problem 3: Equivalent resistance in series

Three resistors of 2 ohms, 3 ohms, and 5 ohms are connected in series.
The equivalent resistance in series is the sum of the individual resistances, which gives 2 plus 3 plus 5 equal to 10 ohms.
The equivalent resistance of the series combination is therefore 10 ohms.

### Problem 4: Equivalent resistance in parallel

Two resistors of 6 ohms and 3 ohms are connected in parallel.
The reciprocal of the equivalent resistance is one divided by 6 plus one divided by 3, which equals one divided by 6 plus two divided by 6 equal to three divided by 6.
The equivalent resistance is therefore 2 ohms.
The equivalent resistance of 2 ohms is smaller than both 6 ohms and 3 ohms.

### Problem 5: Heat produced in a resistor

A current of 2 amperes flows through a resistor of 10 ohms for 30 seconds.
The heat produced is found using H equals I squared multiplied by R multiplied by t, which gives 2 squared multiplied by 10 multiplied by 30.
The heat produced is therefore 4 multiplied by 10 multiplied by 30 equal to 1200 joules.

### Problem 6: Power of an appliance

An electric heater operates on a 220 volt supply and draws a current of 5 amperes.
The power of the heater is found using P equals V multiplied by I, which gives 220 multiplied by 5 equal to 1100 watts.
The power of the heater is therefore 1100 watts, which is equal to 1.1 kilowatts.

### Problem 7: Energy consumed and cost

A 100 watt bulb is used for 10 hours every day for 30 days.
The total time of use is 10 multiplied by 30 equal to 300 hours.
The power of the bulb is 100 watts, which is equal to 0.1 kilowatt.
The energy consumed is power multiplied by time, which gives 0.1 multiplied by 300 equal to 30 kilowatt hours.
The bulb therefore consumes 30 units of electricity in 30 days.
If the cost of one unit is 6 rupees, the total cost is 30 multiplied by 6 equal to 180 rupees.

### Problem 8: Change in resistance when length changes

A wire has a resistance of 8 ohms, and the wire is cut into two equal parts.
Each part has half the length of the original wire, so the resistance of each part is 4 ohms.
When the two parts are joined in parallel, the equivalent resistance is 2 ohms.
When the two parts are joined in series, the equivalent resistance is 8 ohms.

## 18. Common Misconceptions About Electricity

Misconception: Electric current is used up by a bulb as the current passes through the bulb.
Correct fact: The current is the same on both sides of the bulb in a series circuit, because the bulb converts energy and does not use up current.

Misconception: Electrons travel from the battery to the bulb instantly, so the bulb glows because of electrons arriving from the battery.
Correct fact: Free electrons are already present in the wire, and the electric field travels through the wire almost instantly, so the free electrons everywhere in the wire start moving together.

Misconception: Electrons flow in the same direction as the conventional current.
Correct fact: Electrons flow in the direction opposite to the direction of conventional current.

Misconception: A battery is a source of constant current.
Correct fact: A battery is a source of potential difference, and the current depends on the resistance of the circuit.

Misconception: Adding more resistors in parallel increases the total resistance.
Correct fact: Adding more resistors in parallel decreases the total resistance because more paths are available for the current.

Misconception: The resistance of a wire depends on the current flowing through the wire.
Correct fact: The resistance of a wire depends on the length, the area of cross-section, the material, and the temperature of the wire, and not on the current.

Misconception: A thicker wire has more resistance than a thinner wire of the same material and length.
Correct fact: A thicker wire has less resistance than a thinner wire because resistance is inversely proportional to the area of cross-section.

Misconception: Power and energy are the same quantity.
Correct fact: Power is the rate of consuming energy and is measured in watts, whereas energy is the total amount consumed and is measured in joules or kilowatt hours.

Misconception: A kilowatt hour is a unit of power.
Correct fact: A kilowatt hour is a unit of energy because a kilowatt hour is power multiplied by time.

Misconception: An ammeter should be connected in parallel to measure current.
Correct fact: An ammeter must be connected in series, and connecting an ammeter in parallel can damage the ammeter because of its very low resistance.

Misconception: The fuse is connected in the neutral wire.
Correct fact: The fuse is connected in the live wire so that the supply is cut off completely when the fuse blows.

Misconception: Doubling the current doubles the heat produced in a resistor.
Correct fact: Doubling the current makes the heat produced four times as large because heat is proportional to the square of the current.

## 19. Summary of Important Formulas and Units

The formula for current is I equals Q divided by t, and the unit of current is the ampere.
The formula for potential difference is V equals W divided by Q, and the unit of potential difference is the volt.
The formula for Ohm's law is V equals I multiplied by R, and the unit of resistance is the ohm.
The formula for resistance of a wire is R equals rho L divided by A, and the unit of resistivity is the ohm metre.
The formula for resistors in series is R equals R1 plus R2 plus R3, and the current is the same in every resistor.
The formula for resistors in parallel is one divided by R equals one divided by R1 plus one divided by R2 plus one divided by R3, and the potential difference is the same across every resistor.
The formula for heat produced is H equals I squared multiplied by R multiplied by t, and the unit of heat is the joule.
The formula for electric power is P equals V multiplied by I, and the unit of power is the watt.
The formula for electrical energy is E equals P multiplied by t, and the commercial unit of energy is the kilowatt hour.

## 20. Glossary of Key Terms

Electric charge is a property of matter that causes the matter to experience a force in an electric field, and electric charge is measured in coulombs.
Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge, and electric current is measured in amperes.
Potential difference is the work done per unit charge in moving a charge between two points, and potential difference is measured in volts.
Resistance is the opposition offered by a conductor to the flow of current, and resistance is measured in ohms.
Resistivity is the resistance of a conductor of unit length and unit cross-sectional area, and resistivity is measured in ohm metres.
Ohm's law is the statement that potential difference across a conductor is directly proportional to the current when temperature is constant.
Electric power is the rate of consumption of electrical energy, and electric power is measured in watts.
A fuse is a safety device with a low melting point wire that protects a circuit from excess current.
Earthing is the connection of the metal body of an appliance to the ground for safety.
A kilowatt hour is the commercial unit of electrical energy and is equal to 3.6 times 10 to the power 6 joules.
